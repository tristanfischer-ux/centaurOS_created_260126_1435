 Use these six dimensions. They replace the generic “language/narrative” axes with engineering-specific quality gates that remain applicable whether the section is a prose Brief or a tabular Cost Waterfall.

---

### 1. Technical Accuracy
**Core question:** Are the facts, calculations, data, and engineering assertions technically correct and free of error?  
**Why it matters:** Every downstream decision—structural, financial, or programmatic—is built on this layer. Incorrect torque specs or summed totals in a Cost Waterfall compound into catastrophic, expensive failures. Accuracy is the non-negotiable baseline.

### 2. Information Completeness
**Core question:** Does the section contain every mandatory element required by its type (e.g., assumptions, units, contingencies, boundary conditions, risk owners, date ranges)?  
**Why it matters:** Incomplete engineering deliverables force the reader to interpolate, introducing unverified risk. A Risk Register without probability/impact or a Cost Waterfall without escalation assumptions is an engineering liability, not a deliverable.

### 3. Decision Utility
**Core question:** Does this section directly enable a stakeholder to make a committed decision, approve a stage-gate, allocate capital, or change a project trajectory?  
**Why it matters:** Engineering reports are tools, not literature. If consuming the section does not change what the reader does next, it is waste. Value is measured only by the decisions it unlocks.

### 4. Unambiguous Clarity
**Core question:** Is every statement, requirement, and datum precise enough to be interpreted in exactly one way by any qualified reader?  
**Why it matters:** Ambiguity in specs or risk descriptions generates rework, scope disputes, and cascading change orders. In engineering communication, precision is safety. One vague clause costs more than a dozen typos.
